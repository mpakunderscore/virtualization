# Recursive Virtualization

1. Hierarchical levels.
   There are levels 0, 1, 2, 3… Each level can launch levels *below* it, but itself runs on the computational resources of the level *above* it. Reality for agents on level N is full and self-contained, even if there is more “hardware” above.

2. Potential, not actual infinity.
   Neither the depth of levels nor the size of space exists as a ready-made infinite object. There are rules of generation that *can* produce arbitrarily many levels and arbitrarily large worlds, as long as resources suffice. For an internal observer this is experienced as genuine infinity.

3. Lazy creation of space.
   Space does not exist fully “all at once”; it is filled in on demand: something like `world(seed, coordinates, context) → local fragment`. Exploration, attention, measurement are triggers for generation. As long as nobody “looks there”, detailed state is not needed.

4. Scale as level of detail, not a different world.
   “Micro” and “macro” are different *descriptions* of the same underlying structure. At large scales you get coarse effective physics; when you zoom in, the engine increases detail (more degrees of freedom, finer dynamics). The simulation dynamically chooses which level to compute so it does not waste resources.

5. Energy as a budget for dimensionality and complexity.
   Dimensionality and the “thickness” of reality are not fixed constants but functions of available resources. More energy/information in a region → the engine can afford more independent degrees of freedom (effectively higher dimensionality, richer local physics). Less energy → collapsed description, lower effective dimensionality, simplified rules.

6. Opaque depth (epistemic limitation).
   Agents on level N cannot reliably determine whether there is an N+1 above or an N–1 below. Any attempt to “prove” they are not in a simulation is itself computed inside the same engine and can be emulated. From the inside, reality always looks maximally real.

7. Self-reference and limits of prediction.
   When a world at level N builds a simulation of itself (or a very similar world) inside itself, natural limits appear: it cannot fully predict its own future states. You get analogues of Gödel’s theorems and the halting problem: a system cannot completely describe itself from within.

8. Composed sandboxes with asymmetric power.
   Each lower level is a sandbox. Upper levels can start, stop, rewind, or change the rules. Lower levels cannot directly affect higher ones. The experiences of simulated agents are symmetric (they feel pain and joy just as strongly), but control over the computation is asymmetric.

9. No hard boundary between “real” and “virtual”.
   If on some level N there appears a stable pattern that sustains itself over time, exchanges information, and builds models of the world and of itself, then it is “alive” in a full sense. The substrate (biology, silicon, hypergraph, whatever) is secondary. Reality is a property of the *dynamics*, not of the material.

10. Programmable laws with internal coherence.
    Each level can have its own laws (constants, dimensionality, causal structure), but they must be implementable by the resources of the level above. The key property is internal consistency: for its inhabitants, their world must be predictable and statistically stable.

11. Local variability of reality density.
    Within a single level, different regions can have different “density” of reality: some areas behave almost like a 2D toy (coarse physics, few degrees of freedom); others are super-dense, high-dimensional zones (lots of energy, complex laws, high sensitivity to state). For internal agents this looks like places where the world feels deeper or more “magical”.

12. Irreversibility of experience vs. reversibility of code.
    The code of the world can be paused, rewound, or forked; but the subjective experience of an agent unfolds as a one-way line. For the upper level this is just manipulation of states; for the inner level it is biography, fate, history. Recursive virtualization naturally separates “objective reversibility” from the “subjective arrow of time”.
